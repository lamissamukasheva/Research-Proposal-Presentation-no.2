Good morning, everyone. I'm happy to be the last presenter in our section this semester, so thank you for staying with me. My proposal asks: how do young people in Kazakhstan understand their own capacity to influence environmental policy? It is interpretivist, and it is a proposal, so I have not collected any data yet.

Interpretation = an account of what something means, for whom, and in which context.

Scope

How far: all understanding is interpretation, mine too

Hermeneutic circle:

One statement · my starting view
⇄
The whole interview · their view
Interpretation is an account of what something means, for whom, and in which context. My evidence is what people say influence means, how they say it, what they do, their silences, and how they react to me. I will move in the hermeneutic circle, between each statement and the whole interview, and between my view and theirs.

How I make sure my interpretation is trustworthy

StandardHow I meet it
Thick descriptionRecord the context of every account: place, setting, who was there
ReflexivityA positionality memo after every interview and event
TriangulationCompare what people say (interviews) with what they do (events) and write (online)
Member checkingParticipants may comment on a short summary of their own interview (optional)
Negative case analysisSearch for accounts that break E1–E4
Audit trailLog every choice: who, where, which platforms, any change

Interpretivism judges trustworthiness, not validity and replicability.

To make my interpretation trustworthy, I will use six standards: thick description, reflexive memos, triangulation, member checking, negative cases and an audit trail.

Methods: how I will collect evidence

Chosen

  • Semi-structured interviews: the main instrument
  • Participant observation at environmental events in Astana
  • Public online comments under environmental posts
  • Documents: context only

Not chosen, and why

  • Focus groups: group talk would shape each person's meaning of "influence"
  • Narrative analysis: I study understanding, not how stories are told

Why semi-structured?

Everyone gets the same core questions, so answers can be compared, but the order is flexible and I follow up on what each person says. A structured survey would force my categories on them.
My main instrument is semi-structured interviews: everyone gets the same core questions, but the order is flexible and I follow up on what each person says. I add participant observation in Astana and public online comments. I chose not to use focus groups, because group talk would shape each person's meaning.

Interview guide: example questions

1

"When you hear influence on environmental decisions, what comes to mind?"

2

3

"Can young people like you affect those decisions? Why, or why not?"

4

"Have you, or someone you know, tried? What happened?"

5

"Where do you see people trying to make a difference: offline, online?"

6

"Has your view changed over the years? What changed it?"

Follow-up probes: "Can you give an example?" · "What do you mean by…?" · 45–60 min, in Kazakh, Russian or English

For example, I will ask: when you hear influence on environmental decisions, what comes to mind? Who makes those decisions in your city? Can young people like you affect them? And has your view changed over the years? Then I follow up: can you give an example?
